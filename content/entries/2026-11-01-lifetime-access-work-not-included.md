In my [May 2025 update](/2025/05/18/still-clinically-well-adjusted-mostly/), I reported that I had never touched WordPress before trying to build my web store. I also reported spending part of the budget on broken themes and services I didn't end up using. Among the casualties: a $59 promise of “lifetime access.”

A lifetime is a generous amount of time to have access to something you aren't using.

The same account described grant money helping me incorporate and buy tools, word-of-mouth repairs increasing, and a busy workbench. There was work happening. The website was still a beast. Those details belong together, because a stalled website could otherwise swallow the whole story and leave the impression that nothing had moved.

Looking back at that account, the interesting distinction isn't between spending money and refusing to spend it. Some purchases supplied tools for the work. Others supplied access to things that didn't become useful. The receipt records a transaction either way. It cannot tell the difference for you.

That is an awkward problem when the thing you're buying is supposed to help with something you don't yet understand. I was learning WordPress while trying to produce a web store. Knowing what to buy was part of the knowledge I was still acquiring. A product can offer an answer before you've learned to ask a sufficiently specific question.

This is an interpretation of an old account, not evidence that I've since conquered the problem. The archive doesn't establish exactly why each purchase failed, whether any service later became useful, or what happened to that particular $59. I'm not going to hold a trial for an unnamed theme on the strength of one paragraph.

But the paragraph leaves a useful question: what, precisely, does this purchase remove from the work?

If the obstacle is missing equipment, a tool may remove it. If the obstacle is not knowing what to do next, access alone may leave you standing in the same place, now with a login. The work has acquired an account rather than lost an obstacle.

I wouldn't turn that into a rule against buying things. The workbench in that same update is a good reason not to. I'd keep it as a question worth asking before the next receipt: am I getting what I need to do the work, or something that makes the unfinished work look better equipped?

“Lifetime” tells me how long the door is open. It doesn't tell me whether I've found the right door.
