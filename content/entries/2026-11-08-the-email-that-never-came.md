In [December 2024](/2024/12/18/synergies-works-blaze-accelerator/), I wrote about finishing a nine-week business accelerator. Pitch Day allowed four minutes to present the business and two minutes for questions. My review of my own delivery involved a 1980s monotone robot and the possibility of putting the audience to sleep. Apparently, even my imaginary replacement needed presentation coaching.

But another detail in that account interests me more than the pitch.

I had missed a few sessions. I wrote that I kept waiting for the “We regret to inform you…” email. It never came.

There is a whole little courtroom inside those two sentences. In the first, I'm already anticipating the verdict. In the second, the people supposedly delivering it haven't done so. The prosecution appears to be working independently.

That is a reading of the old account, not an explanation of anybody else's intentions. I don't know why no such email came. I joked then that perhaps they saw potential, or perhaps they had forgotten to update the roster. Neither joke constitutes access to anybody else's inner life.

What the account does establish is less complicated: I missed sessions, expected dismissal, and still reached the end of the program. Alongside the awkward pitch, I described gratitude, confidence, and people who had become important to me. The imperfect attendance was part of that experience. It wasn't the entire experience.

I wouldn't erase the missed sessions to improve the story. Nor would I promote the anticipated rejection into something that actually happened. Both edits would make the record less honest, although one would sound encouraging and the other might sound painfully familiar.

The useful question is where responsibility ends and prediction begins. Missing something is an event. Deciding what that absence means to everyone else is another operation entirely. It may feel like facing facts when it is really writing the next scene without consulting the other characters.

None of this establishes that attendance doesn't matter, or that every group will make room for the same difficulties. This was one program, at one point in my life. Its actual requirements and the reasons for its decisions aren't preserved in that post.

Still, the distinction is worth keeping. I could acknowledge what I'd missed without treating an unwritten dismissal as an official document.

The final pitch was imperfect too, at least by my own very uncharitable review. Yet the same account says I would do the program again a thousand times. An experience can be worth returning to even when you wouldn't care to replay every minute of your performance.

Especially the robot minutes.
