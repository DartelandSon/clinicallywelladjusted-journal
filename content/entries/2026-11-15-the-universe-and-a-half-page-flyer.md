In [November 2025](/2025/11/30/on-kicking-rocks-and-bruising-butts/), I put two ambitions in the same post: helping humanity reach the farthest parts of the universe, and finishing a half-page flyer.

The universe was still on the agenda. The flyer was winning.

I described it as already 99% complete, then admitted I had procrastinated by writing the post instead. I attributed the difficulty to ADHD. That is the account preserved there; it isn't a report on what I finished afterward. The archive doesn't supply a triumphant photograph of the flyer, much less a launch date for my interstellar civilization.

What interests me about that pairing is how poorly the size of an ambition predicts the difficulty of its next task. A half-page advertisement sounds embarrassingly small beside the cosmos. But embarrassment doesn't finish advertisements. Neither does pointing out that they are small.

The old post makes a joke out of the distance between what I wanted and what I could get done that day. The joke works because both ends of it were real in the account. I didn't have to pretend my ambitions were modest to admit that a modest task had beaten me.

There is also a trap in that 99%. It describes the amount of flyer already there. It says very little about what finishing required. The remaining one percent might be tiny on the page and still be the entire difference between an almost-finished advertisement and one somebody could use.

That isn't a diagnosis of the unfinished part. I didn't record enough detail to explain precisely what stopped me, and I won't reverse-engineer an explanation from a percentage. It is a reason to be suspicious of the word “just” when it appears before somebody else's remaining work. Just finish it. Just send it. Just do the last little bit. An excellent collection of instructions for someone whose problem is not knowing that the task exists.

I wrote a blog post instead. That produced something worth keeping, but it didn't establish that the flyer was done. Both facts can stay in the record without making the detour either a secret productivity technique or proof that the larger ambitions were fraudulent.

The unanswered question is smaller, and probably more useful, than whether I deserved those ambitions: what would have made that particular last step possible?

The universe can remain enormous. The next step needs a better description than “only one percent.”
